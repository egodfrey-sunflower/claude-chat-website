Glossary of trees, flowers and shrubs

Some of the most interesting plants of the British Isles: how to tell them apart, and the history, folklore and ecology that make them worth knowing. Botanical terms are at the bottom.

Words worth knowing

Ancient woodland
In England and Wales, land that has been continuously wooded since at least 1600 (1750 in Scotland). It covers only a few per cent of Britain but holds a disproportionate share of its woodland wildlife.
Ancient woodland indicator
A plant that spreads so slowly, or needs such undisturbed soil, that finding it suggests a wood is old. Examples are wood anemone, bluebell, small-leaved lime and wild garlic. It's a clue, not a proof.
Archaeophyte
A plant brought to Britain by people before 1500, often with early farming. Examples are the common poppy and many cornfield weeds. Later arrivals are neophytes.
Catkin
A dangling spike of tiny, petal-less flowers, usually wind-pollinated. Hazel, birch, alder and willow all have them.
Coppicing
Cutting a tree to near ground level so that it regrows as many straight poles, which are harvested again every 7–25 years. Coppicing can keep a tree alive far longer than it would otherwise live. Hazel, ash and sweet chestnut were the classic coppice crops.
Hemiparasite
A plant that photosynthesises for itself but also taps another plant for water and nutrients. Examples are mistletoe, yellow rattle and eyebright.
Heterostyly
Having two or more flower forms with the stigma and anthers at different heights, which encourages cross-pollination. The primrose's "pin" and "thrum" flowers are the classic case. Darwin wrote a book about it.
Marcescence
Keeping dead leaves on the branch through winter. You see it in young beech and hornbeam, and in clipped beech hedges.
Pinnate
A compound leaf made of leaflets arranged in pairs along a central stalk, like a feather. Ash, rowan and elder have pinnate leaves.
Pollard
Like coppicing, but cut at head height, out of reach of grazing animals. Old pollards are some of our most ancient trees.

This is a general-interest guide, not a foraging manual. Several plants here are seriously poisonous (and some look like edible ones), and many wild plants are protected. Only pick or eat what you have identified with certainty and are allowed to take. Never dig up wild plants.
